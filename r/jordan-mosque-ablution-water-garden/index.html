Jordan mosque turns prayer washing water into a garden in the desert

10 Nov 2026, 05:00 GMT · London
In one of the driest countries on earth, a mosque in the Jordan valley filters the water worshippers use before prayer and sends it to its trees and flowerbeds.

A mosque in Deir Alla, in the Jordan valley west of Amman, has become an unlikely green patch in a landscape of bare hills, thanks to water that would otherwise be lost down the drain.

The Abu Obeida mosque collects the water worshippers use for wudu, the ritual washing of hands, face and feet before each of the five daily prayers. Pipes beneath the washing area carry it to a treatment unit, where it passes through layers of gravel and sand. Because no soap is used, natural settling is enough to clean it, and the water then feeds drip lines running through the trees and flowerbeds outside.

The scale of the problem explains the effort. Jordan has only about 61 cubic metres of renewable fresh water per person each year, far below the United Nations threshold for absolute scarcity. Piped supply in some areas arrives for as little as 12 hours a week, and households rely on rooftop tanks. The country has more than 7,600 mosques.

The local conservation group running the scheme has worked on the idea since 2008 but has reached only 18 mosques and schools, held back by cost, technical hurdles and confusion over whether imams or caretakers should maintain the equipment. The religious affairs ministry says more than 35 mosques now reuse washing water for irrigation or cleaning, more than 50 harvest rainwater, and well over a thousand preachers and muezzins have been trained to promote water saving.

Not everyone is convinced. A former irrigation minister notes that Jordan already reuses around 150 million cubic metres of treated wastewater a year and says the volumes from ablutions are smaller than people imagine. Worshippers also admit that a Friday sermon on saving water does not always stop people leaving taps running at home.
