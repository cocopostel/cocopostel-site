Seven in ten Americans want the new Congress to end the Iran war

10 Nov 2026, 20:07 GMT · London
A national survey finds broad backing for legislation to stop the war, repeal tariffs and tackle living costs, but little appetite for impeaching the president.

A large majority of Americans want the Congress that takes office in January to pass a law ending the war with Iran, according to a national poll released on Tuesday.

The survey found 71% support for such legislation, including 93% of Democrats and a narrow majority of Republicans at 51%. Around 62% favoured scrapping the president's import tariffs, and nine in ten said lawmakers should make the cost of living their main focus in their first 100 days.

The findings link voters' economic worries directly to the administration's foreign and trade policy, since both the war and the tariffs have added to pressure on household budgets.

Respondents also wanted the two sides to cooperate. Clear majorities in both parties said it mattered that President Donald Trump work with Democrats and that Democratic leaders try to work with him. Retribution ranked lower: 42% said an attempt to impeach the president would harm the country, against 33% who thought it would help.

Turning those wishes into law will not be simple. Democrats are set to hold between 54 and 56 Senate seats depending on uncalled races in Mississippi and Alaska, short of the 60 needed to beat a filibuster, and the president retains his veto. Reversing tariffs or other policies would need Republican votes or a direct confrontation with the White House.

The poll also showed the Democratic Party itself remains unpopular, viewed favourably by 45% and unfavourably by 51%. A row over leadership is building. Hakeem Jeffries is expected to become Speaker despite an online petition against him that has passed a million signatures, with House leadership votes due on 18 and 19 November. In the Senate, Chuck Schumer faces pressure to stand aside, and Maryland senator Chris Van Hollen is considering a challenge.

Critics on the left accuse both men of being too close to corporate donors and pro Israel lobbying groups and of failing to confront the administration. Some analysts argue, though, that congressional leaders are judged mainly on managing their caucus, and that the role of party figurehead will fall to the 2028 presidential hopefuls.
