Hakeem Jeffries stands on the brink of the House speakership

07 Nov 2026, 13:50 GMT · London
After the Democratic midterm sweep, the New York leader is set to become the first Black speaker, even as progressives press him over Trump, immigration enforcement and Israel.

House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries of New York is poised to become the first Black speaker of the US House of Representatives after Democrats' decisive midterm victory.

The fifty six year old Brooklyn raised congressman would take a central role in efforts to constrain President Donald Trump in the final two years of his term. Yet some in his party doubt whether his cautious style and long record of support for Israel fit the moment.

An online petition urging lawmakers to pick someone else had gathered more than nine hundred and eighty thousand signatures by Saturday. Several newly elected progressive members have not said whether they will back him. Critics argue the speaker should answer Democratic voters rather than the party establishment alone.

Jeffries, the son of social workers from Crown Heights, worked as a corporate lawyer before winning a New York Assembly seat in 2006 and a House seat six years later. He helped broker a bipartisan criminal justice law signed by Trump in 2018, served as an impeachment manager in 2020, and in 2022 became the first Black politician to lead a party in either chamber when he succeeded Nancy Pelosi as House Democratic leader.

Where Pelosi was known for top down command, Jeffries has built a quieter consensus style. That has frustrated colleagues who want a harder line against Trump. After the election he said Americans want results rather than revenge, a line that drew sharp pushback from the left.

Progressive newcomers, sometimes called The Bloc, had floated using the speaker vote to win concessions on health care, ICE funding and weapons shipments to Israel. A comfortable Democratic majority now makes Jeffries less dependent on those votes, but also removes his old argument that Republican control blocked accountability.

His ties to pro Israel donors and past opposition to conditioning aid remain a deep divide. In July he voted against cutting annual military aid to Israel yet wrote that a meaningful change in US Israel relations was needed. No serious rival for the speakership has emerged, so the larger test will come once he holds the gavel.
