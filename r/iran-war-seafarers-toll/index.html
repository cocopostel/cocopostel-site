At least 25 seafarers dead as Iran war turns Hormuz into a deadly passage

09 Nov 2026, 07:07 GMT · London
Merchant crews from far beyond the region are paying a heavy price as attacks on shipping around the Strait of Hormuz continue nine months into the war.

Civilian sailors have become some of the quieter casualties of the war on Iran. The International Maritime Organization has counted at least 25 seafarers killed since the fighting began on 28 February, and at least 129 incidents involving commercial ships in and around the Strait of Hormuz.

The waterway once carried roughly a fifth of the oil the world consumes. After the United States and Israel opened their campaign, Iran effectively shut the strait and warned that ships moving without its permission could be hit, while Washington placed a naval blockade on Iranian ports. Vessels have since been struck by missiles, projectiles and sea mines, leaving some ablaze and others on the seabed.

The pace has not slowed. October was the worst month so far, with at least 31 ships hit. Last Thursday Iranian forces reported fires aboard five tankers and a tug in separate incidents, and on Sunday the Revolutionary Guard said three tankers it accused of using an unauthorised route had suffered blasts and serious fires. A tanker flagged in the Marshall Islands also exploded north of Qatar, killing one crew member.

Indian nationals have suffered most, with at least 16 dead. Three were killed in June in American strikes on merchant ships near Oman, which led New Delhi to summon a senior US diplomat, and a sailor from Gujarat died last week when his cargo vessel was hit off the Omani coast, although 13 shipmates were rescued. Two Filipino crew members died in September aboard a Saudi tanker, and an Iranian maritime union said in May that at least 44 Iranian seafarers had been killed.

Shipping industry representatives say crews going about their work have been put in harm's way through no fault of their own, and that the strain on mental health is growing. The maritime body said in late summer that about 6,000 sailors were stuck near the strait, raising wider questions about whether crews can refuse dangerous voyages and what protection international law really offers them.
