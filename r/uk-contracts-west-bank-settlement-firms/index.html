Britain keeps awarding public contracts to firms linked to West Bank settlements

10 Nov 2026, 05:39 GMT · London
Public bodies have handed £10.7m in new work to companies tied to Israeli settlements since London promised to sanction those who profit from them.

British public bodies have signed off £10.7m worth of new contracts with companies connected to Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank, even after the government vowed to punish firms that profit from settlement activity, a new investigation has found.

On 8 September the Foreign Secretary, Ed Miliband, told MPs that Britain would act against companies and individuals supplying construction, infrastructure, financing or property services for settlement growth, and warned that those who back the settlements would feel the full weight of UK sanctions. Settlements are illegal under international law.

Since that statement, however, details of 18 further contracts with linked companies have been published. The firms involved are named in a United Nations database of businesses tied to settlement activity, or are subsidiaries owned by such businesses.

The largest group in the latest batch is Heidelberg Materials, the German building materials company. Three of its subsidiaries won 14 contracts worth £10.4m, mostly for road resurfacing for National Highways and for councils in Nottingham and Lincolnshire. Its Israeli arm owns a quarry south of Qalqilya on land belonging to Palestinian villages. The company has said the quarry stopped operating in 2023, but approval to expand the site was granted in May 2025.

Motorola Solutions, also listed by the UN, won two small new police communications deals and remains by far the biggest holder of such contracts, worth about £1.7bn in total, largely through the Airwave network used by emergency services across Great Britain. Smaller awards went to subsidiaries of Fosun International and the French engineering group Egis, which disputes its inclusion on the UN list.

A legal scholar said the firms appeared to be breaching their obligations under international humanitarian law. The former Labour leader Jeremy Corbyn called the continued flow of public money outrageous. The Cabinet Office did not respond, but has previously said individual public authorities decide on suppliers case by case and that procurement should not be used to boycott suppliers unless formal sanctions apply.
