Satellites and rockets become a new battleground in the Russia Ukraine war

08 Nov 2026, 05:00 GMT · London
Ukrainian strikes on Russian space industry sites, Moscow's push for its own satellite network and orbital harassment show how the war has spread beyond the earth.

The war between Russia and Ukraine is increasingly being fought over access to space, as both sides race to secure the satellites that guide drones, sensors and communications on the battlefield.

In the early hours of 15 August, Ukrainian missiles struck the Progress Rocket and Space Centre in the Volga city of Samara, the maker of Russia's Soyuz rockets. Two buildings were hit, among them a clean room where circuit boards for the launch systems were assembled. The factory builds the rockets that carry Russia's new Rassvet satellites, a network Moscow is rushing into orbit after it was cut off from Elon Musk's Starlink earlier this year.

Ukraine has also struck other sites linked to the Russian space agency this year, including the Korolyov complex north of Moscow and a radio telescope in occupied Crimea that was originally built by Ukraine.

Russia, for its part, has fired its Oreshnik ballistic missile at least three times. The weapon climbs to the edge of space before releasing several warheads that can strike separate targets. That threat, together with recent limits on Starlink service, has pushed Kyiv to reduce its reliance on American systems. Ukraine has run at least three tests of interceptions at very high altitude, and last month it launched a first test satellite with a Danish partner for a planned national communications network that could eventually number about 245 satellites.

Tensions in orbit are rising more widely. In September the US air force secretary acknowledged for the first time that Washington had placed space control weapons in orbit. Weeks later a Russian satellite was seen manoeuvring aggressively towards a commercial Finnish radar satellite that supplies terrain imaging to Ukraine's military, a move experts described as deliberate harassment that could force the target to burn scarce fuel.

Specialists say the treaties meant to govern space are vague, dated and hard to enforce. One Swedish defence academic argued that warfare in orbit is no longer a distant prospect but a present reality.
